Why pictures can make a new language easier to understand

Before you translate a word, look at what’s happening.

A raised eyebrow. A locked door. An empty glass. Someone running away. Visual information gives language context — another set of clues that can help you work out what you’re reading.

But do pictures actually help us learn a language? Research suggests they can — particularly when visual information is combined with words rather than used as a replacement for them.

Words and pictures can work better together.

A 2025 meta-analysis examined multimedia second-language vocabulary learning across studies involving 2,056 participants. It compared learning with first-language information, pictures, and combinations of the two.

For immediate vocabulary learning, combining first-language words with pictures performed better than using either first-language information or pictures alone. The advantage was less clear in delayed testing, so the research shouldn’t be interpreted as saying that adding a picture automatically guarantees better long-term learning.

Source Zhang, C., Sala, G. & Gobet, F. (2025), Effectiveness of L1 and pictures in multimedia conditions on learning second-language vocabulary: A meta-analysis, Educational Research Review, 47, 100681.

Pictures aren’t magic.

The research is more interesting than simply saying “pictures are better.” The same 2025 meta-analysis found no significant difference overall between learning with first-language information alone and learning with pictures alone. The strongest immediate results came from combining the two.

That matters. A useful visual shouldn’t simply decorate a word. It can become another source of information that works alongside the language.

A comic gives you more than a picture. It gives you a situation.

On its own, you have only the words.

Now imagine those words inside a comic panel: one character looks frightened, another is reaching towards a door, and something appears to be waiting outside. Before reaching for a translation, the scene has already started narrowing down what the sentence might mean.

That’s different from simply putting a picture next to a vocabulary word. A story supplies characters, actions, expressions, objects and consequences. The learner is using language inside a situation.

The words can help you when you need them.

Visual context won’t always be enough. Sometimes you need to know exactly what a word means. That’s where interactive reading can add another layer.

A 2024 meta-analysis brought together 21 studies of second- and foreign-language vocabulary learning through digital reading. Overall, digital reading was associated with significant vocabulary-learning effects, although results varied according to factors such as learner proficiency, testing method and the digital resources available.

One particularly relevant finding was the usefulness of lexical glosses — explanations of unfamiliar words available while reading.

Source Zhu, T., Zhang, Y. & Irwin, D. (2024), Second and Foreign Language Vocabulary Learning through Digital Reading: A Meta-Analysis, Education and Information Technologies, 29, 4531–4563.
